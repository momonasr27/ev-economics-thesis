# Thesis summary

**Title:** *Viaggio nelle auto elettriche: una prospettiva economica su innovazione e investimenti* (A journey into electric vehicles: an economic perspective on innovation and investment)
**Degree:** Bachelor's in Business Administration (Economia e Gestione Aziendale), Roma Tre University
**Supervisor:** Prof. Fabio Giulio Grandis
**Academic year:** 2024–2025

## Introduction

Electric cars are not a recent invention: the first experiments date back to the 19th century. Around 1900 they competed with petrol cars, but cheap oil and better combustion engines pushed them out of the market. The oil crises of the 1970s and the rise of environmental movements brought them back. Lithium-ion batteries have now made long ranges possible.

The thesis asks three questions: what the EV market looks like today, whether EVs are already an established reality, and what the future holds, including possible investment opportunities.

## Chapter I: The EV market

**Production.** An EV has far fewer mechanical parts than a combustion car, but it is not cheaper to build. The main reason is the **battery**, which accounts for about 40% of the production cost. The electric motor and powertrain account for about 20%, and the lightweight body and robotised assembly for about 15%.

**Lithium-ion batteries.** Lithium-ion is the dominant battery technology (2019 Nobel Prize in Chemistry), thanks to its high energy density, low weight and fast charging. Its weaknesses are degradation over time, fire risk from the liquid electrolyte, and cost (about €100–200 per kWh).

**Lithium, the "new oil".** Lithium comes from hard-rock mines (mainly Australia) and from brines in the "lithium triangle" (Chile, Argentina, Bolivia), which holds over half of the world's deposits. Prices collapsed from about $85,000/t at the 2022 peak to $13,500/t in 2023. China produces about 92% of the world's batteries and has secured supply from foreign mines. The thesis also mentions concerns about forced-labour risks in the supply chain.

**Sales.** China leads clearly: EVs are about to outsell combustion cars there. Europe is slowing down, with the Nordic countries far ahead (Norway 91.6% of new car sales) and Italy far behind (about 9%). EU carmakers face stricter CO₂ targets from 2025, and the EU has imposed tariffs of up to 35.3% on Chinese EVs. In the US, EV sales reached 1.3 million in 2024 (8.1% market share), but federal policy under the new administration may slow the transition.

## Chapter II: Business and investment

**Going public.** The chapter compares the two main routes to a stock-market listing:
- **IPO** (initial public offering): longer and more expensive, but with stronger investor guarantees. Tesla (2010), NIO (2018) and BYD went public this way.
- **SPAC** (special purpose acquisition company): faster and cheaper, but historically with weaker returns for investors. Polestar went public this way.

**Main listed EV makers.**
- **Tesla:** brand leader and technology pioneer (autonomous driving, robotaxis), but with premium prices and a CEO whose public statements move the stock.
- **BYD:** the world's largest EV producer and a major battery supplier. It competes on price: its Seagull is one of the cheapest EVs on the market.
- **NIO:** competes on service (long warranties, free roadside assistance) and on performance.

**Investment idea: charging infrastructure.** Charging points are the "glue" of the EV transition. Range anxiety and the habit of keeping the battery above 60% increase the number of chargers needed. In 2022 there were about 3 million public charging points worldwide, most of them in China. Bloomberg expects the charging-infrastructure market to reach about $44bn in 2025 and to grow at a 25.94% CAGR until 2030. The thesis presents it as a promising investment area.

## Chapter III: China's dominance and the Italian case

**China.** From 2018 to 2022, China invested $329bn in clean technologies, against $29bn for Europe and the US combined. It controls raw-material supply and competes on low prices. In 2023, 63% of the 10.4 million EVs sold worldwide were made in China.

**How the West responds.** The main response is tariffs, but they can be partly circumvented, for example by producing in Mexico for the North American market. The US is moving towards restrictions on Chinese connected-car software and hardware on national-security grounds. Europe risks Chinese overcapacity being redirected to its market. Mario Draghi has called for faster, coordinated EU action.

**Italy.** Italy lags behind most of Europe. Its EV fleet is concentrated in the North (Lombardy, Emilia-Romagna, Veneto). The main barriers are high purchase and charging costs, uncertain incentives and infrastructure gaps (about 60,000 public charging points). Yet consumer interest is high: 65% of people surveyed in 28 countries (EY, 2024) plan to buy an EV within two years. In Q1 2025, Italian EV registrations grew by 75% year on year.

## Conclusions

The future of mobility is electric. Prices will move closer to those of combustion cars, and performance is already better in some respects (for example acceleration). The main obstacle remains the purchase price. The transition requires investment in infrastructure and incentives, and the West can learn from China's integrated, supply-chain-driven approach.
